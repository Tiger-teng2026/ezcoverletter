In 2026, most software engineering applications still pass through an applicant tracking system before a human ever opens them. Recruiters and hiring managers skim fast. Your cover letter has one job: prove you match the stack, the problem space, and the outcomes the role cares about—without sounding like a keyword dump.

## Start from the job description, not your resume dump

Open the posting and highlight:

- Languages and frameworks (TypeScript, Go, React, Kubernetes)
- Domains (payments, growth, developer tools, ML infra)
- Signals of seniority (ownership, system design, mentorship, on-call)

Your first paragraph should name the role and mirror 2–3 of those signals in plain English. Example shape: *I’m applying for the Backend Engineer role on the Payments team—I’ve shipped Go services that cut p99 latency and owned PCI-adjacent APIs end to end.*

## Match tech-stack keywords the way ATS expects

ATS tools are literal. If the posting says “PostgreSQL,” write “PostgreSQL,” not only “relational databases.” If it says “CI/CD,” say “CI/CD” and briefly show which pipeline you used.

Do:

- Reuse the employer’s exact product/stack phrasing where it is true for you
- Place keywords in full sentences, not comma-separated laundry lists
- Prioritize the stack the role repeats most often

Don’t:

- Invent tools you have not used
- Paste your entire skills section into the letter
- Force every buzzword from the JD into one paragraph

## Quantify engineering work so humans and parsers both win

Soft claims (“strong problem solver”) die in screening. Prefer outcomes:

- *Reduced checkout API p95 from 420ms to 180ms by rewriting the hot path in Go and adding caching*
- *Cut CI wall time 35% by parallelizing Jest suites and caching Docker layers*
- *Led migration of 12 services to Kubernetes with zero customer-facing downtime*

Use numbers, scope, and timeboxes. ATS may not “score” metrics cleverly, but recruiters do—and metric-rich sentences still contain the keywords that help you clear filters.

## Keep structure ATS-safe

Use a simple layout:

1. Role + fit in one tight opening
2. One paragraph of stack-aligned proof
3. One paragraph of ownership / collaboration / reliability
4. A short close with availability for a screen

Avoid tables, multi-column layouts, icons, and text boxes. Those break parsing more often than they impress anyone.
