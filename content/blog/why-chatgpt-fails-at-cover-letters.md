ChatGPT is excellent at drafting. It is much weaker at producing a cover letter that is simultaneously human, ATS-safe, and tightly matched to one job. If you paste a resume and a vague prompt into a general chatbot, you usually get something that *looks* finished—and still fails quietly in screening.

## The “sounds AI” problem is real

Recruiters have seen hundreds of ChatGPT letters. Common tells:

- Generic openers (“I am writing to express my interest…”)
- Soft adjective stacks (“passionate,” “dedicated,” “results-driven”) with little proof
- The same middle paragraph structure for every company
- Confident claims that do not match the resume

ATS may not care about tone, but humans do. A letter that reads like default ChatGPT often gets discarded in the first skim—even when your experience is strong.

## Format and structure break more often than people expect

General chat tools optimize for readable chat replies, not export-ready application documents. That leads to:

- Odd spacing, markdown leftovers, or uneven paragraph breaks when you copy/paste
- Sign-off blocks that duplicate or leave placeholders
- Headings, bullets, or emphasis styles that look fine in chat and messy in Word/PDF/email
- Content that drifts into resume-summary mode instead of a tight one-page letter

ATS parsers prefer simple, linear text. Fancy or inconsistent formatting from a chat transcript is a quiet rejection risk.

## Keyword matching is not “write me a cover letter”

Passing ATS usually requires deliberate alignment with the posting: role title, tools, domain language, and outcomes. Raw ChatGPT will invent plausible synonyms, skip repeated JD phrases, or bury the exact terms the filter expects.

A purpose-built flow forces the inputs that matter—your resume plus the actual job description—and keeps the output in a cover-letter shape instead of an essay.
